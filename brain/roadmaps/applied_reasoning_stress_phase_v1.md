# Brain Applied Reasoning Stress Phase v1

## Purpose

Brain Applied Reasoning Stress Phase v1 stress-tests Brain reasoning quality against difficult real-world pricing systems before any production expansion.

The Brain architecture now has sufficient internal governance and ontology structure to reason coherently inside its own documents. The next risk is not missing architecture. The next risk is insufficient collision with difficult pricing systems that expose weak analogies, ontology stretch, retrieval failure, intervention overclaim, false causal transfer, hidden pricing-logic differences, and governance weaknesses under real reasoning pressure.

This phase is documentation-only. It creates a roadmap for manual reasoning stress tests. It does not create production schema fields, ontology labels, automation, recommendation logic, navigator intelligence, public UI, hidden JSON, databases, or production case behavior.

## Why This Phase Is Needed

Internal coherence is not the same as external collision. A Brain architecture can be orderly, well-governed, and internally consistent while still failing when cases contain messy buyer incentives, multi-party economics, dynamic capacity constraints, hidden commitments, or ambiguous value metrics.

Ontology elegance is a risk. A clean ontology can make hard cases look simpler than they are. If the Brain maps every difficult system into familiar concepts too quickly, it may preserve elegance while losing explanatory power.

False analogy risk increases as the case library grows. Two systems can share pricing vocabulary while differing in bill trigger, risk transfer, buyer control, seller economics, or value realization. Similar-looking systems should not be treated as transferable without mechanism-level discrimination.

Retrieval can become overconfident. A retrieved case is useful only when it clarifies reasoning. It is harmful when it sounds related but hides the causal differences that should have limited transfer.

Intervention simulation can drift. A simulated pricing change can sound like a recommendation even when evidence status remains hypothesized. Realistic cases must pressure the Brain to name trade-offs, harmed segments, seller-side risk, leading indicators, and failure signals.

Architecture entropy can emerge from insufficient reality testing. If the Brain responds to every awkward case by creating a new concept, protocol, exception, or ontology label, the architecture will grow before it has learned which problems repeat.

Governance systems need edge cases. Brain review, concept lifecycle, retrieval policy, analogy discipline, ontology fit, and intervention simulation should encounter enough difficult cases to reveal where they are useful, where they are vague, and where they invite overclaim.

## Phase Goals

- Improve causal discrimination across pricing systems that look similar on the surface.
- Improve analogy discipline by identifying what transfers, what does not transfer, and why.
- Improve ontology boundary clarity without expanding ontology labels from isolated cases.
- Improve retrieval usefulness by judging whether retrieved cases make reasoning clearer.
- Improve intervention honesty by exposing expected upside, downside, trade-off, and evidence limits.
- Improve reasoning failure visibility by documenting where the Brain misclassifies, overclaims, or retrieves poorly.
- Identify repeated ontology gaps without premature ontology expansion.

## Hard Case Expansion

### Dynamic Repricing Systems

Candidate examples:

- airline yield pricing
- hotel pricing
- cloud spot pricing
- Ticketmaster/event pricing
- Uber surge pricing

Why difficult:
Dynamic repricing systems change prices through time, capacity, demand, inventory, or market-clearing pressure. The buyer may face the same product at different prices depending on when, where, or under what demand conditions they buy.

Ontology weakness pressured:
These cases pressure whether the ontology can separate price level changes from bill-trigger logic, capacity rationing, willingness-to-pay extraction, demand smoothing, and risk transfer. They also pressure whether "dynamic pricing" is being used as a precise mechanism or a vague label for any changing price.

Likely analogy mistakes:
The Brain may flatten airline yield pricing, hotel rates, cloud spot instances, event ticket pricing, and surge pricing into one dynamic repricing archetype. That would hide critical differences in inventory perishability, buyer urgency, transparency, substitutability, seller control, and whether the buyer can defer demand.

Intervention reasoning risks:
Interventions such as price visibility, caps, notifications, or smoothing can improve perceived fairness while weakening allocation efficiency, reducing supply responsiveness, or shifting risk back to the seller. The Brain must not assume that making dynamic pricing clearer always improves trust or conversion.

### Bundled Entitlement Systems

Candidate examples:

- telecom bundles
- Microsoft 365
- Amazon Prime
- Playstation Plus
- Apple One
- Costco membership

Why difficult:
Bundled entitlement systems combine access, usage rights, services, content, savings, rewards, or status into one purchase boundary. The buyer often pays one visible price while the realized value depends on which entitlements they notice, use, or value.

Ontology weakness pressured:
These cases pressure bundle boundary reasoning. The Brain must distinguish payment cadence, access rights, usage allowances, service benefits, reward offsets, and renewal logic instead of treating every recurring bundle as the same pricing mechanism.

Likely analogy mistakes:
The Brain may overgeneralize from one bundle to another because all appear to package multiple benefits. It may compare Amazon Prime to Microsoft 365 or Costco to Apple One without separating operational fulfillment, software access, household sharing, content rights, rewards, and retail savings.

Intervention reasoning risks:
Interventions that improve transparency can expose underuse, weak entitlement fit, or low realized value. Bundle simplification can improve comprehension while reducing perceived breadth. Benefit recaps can improve renewal among high-use members while increasing cancellation or downgrade among low-use buyers.

### Marketplace And Two-Sided Monetization

Candidate examples:

- Visa/Mastercard
- App Store fees
- Google Ads
- TikTok marketplace
- Airbnb
- Shopify
- restaurant delivery marketplaces

Why difficult:
Marketplace and two-sided systems monetize interactions between multiple parties. The buyer who pays may not be the only actor whose behavior matters, and fees may be charged to sellers, advertisers, developers, merchants, consumers, or intermediaries.

Ontology weakness pressured:
These cases pressure whether the Brain can track who pays, who benefits, who is subsidized, who bears risk, and which side of the market the pricing mechanism is optimizing. They also test whether the Brain can distinguish take rate, auction pricing, transaction fees, service fees, and platform access.

Likely analogy mistakes:
The Brain may treat all marketplace fees as the same because they are platform-mediated. That hides differences between payment networks, app distribution, advertising auctions, creator marketplaces, merchant enablement, lodging marketplaces, and delivery platforms.

Intervention reasoning risks:
Reducing or increasing one side's fee can shift prices, supply, demand, quality, fraud risk, seller participation, or platform investment. The Brain must avoid assuming fee cuts pass through cleanly to end users or that platform take rate changes affect all marketplace participants symmetrically.

### Hybrid Monetization Systems

Candidate examples:

- OpenAI API + ChatGPT Plus
- Snowflake
- Datadog
- Nvidia DGX Cloud
- Microsoft 365
- Google Ads

Why difficult:
Hybrid monetization systems combine subscriptions, usage meters, commitments, credits, platform access, add-ons, enterprise negotiation, and service tiers. The same customer may encounter multiple bill-changing logics at once.

Ontology weakness pressured:
These cases pressure whether the Brain can handle layered monetized units without collapsing them into one dominant label. They also test whether commitment pricing, usage exposure, seat-based access, overage, prepaid credits, and enterprise terms can be reasoned about together without creating premature schema expansion.

Likely analogy mistakes:
The Brain may compare usage-based API pricing to cloud consumption pricing or SaaS subscriptions without distinguishing compute cost pass-through, capacity commitments, observability volume, user seats, model choice, and buyer predictability needs.

Intervention reasoning risks:
Commitment discounts, clearer usage alerts, prepaid credits, or plan packaging can improve predictability while reducing flexibility, increasing lock-in concerns, weakening consumption growth, or changing seller revenue visibility. The Brain must name which risk moves and who absorbs it.

### Operational Burden And Hidden Fee Systems

Candidate examples:

- healthcare reimbursement
- insurance deductibles
- restaurant delivery marketplaces
- telecom bundles
- Ticketmaster/event pricing
- hotel pricing

Why difficult:
Operational burden and hidden fee systems often create buyer cost through timing, eligibility, claims, service boundaries, convenience fees, deductibles, taxes, surcharges, or post-selection add-ons. The visible price may not be the final experienced cost.

Ontology weakness pressured:
These cases pressure whether the Brain can separate list price from realized price, buyer effort, service boundary, reimbursement logic, fee visibility, eligibility rules, and risk exposure. They also test whether hidden friction is treated as part of pricing logic rather than merely communication.

Likely analogy mistakes:
The Brain may group hidden fees together without distinguishing pass-through fees, platform fees, regulatory fees, insurance cost-sharing, reimbursement uncertainty, service convenience, and operational burden.

Intervention reasoning risks:
Making costs visible earlier may improve trust while reducing conversion. Simplifying fees may require price increases elsewhere. Reducing operational burden can increase seller cost or change incentives. The Brain must not assume that transparency alone resolves pricing harm.

## Cross-Case Comparative Reasoning

The Brain must evolve from:

```text
one case -> one ontology mapping
```

toward:

```text
case A vs case B vs case C discrimination reasoning
```

The goal is not only to map a case to concepts. The goal is to explain why similar-looking cases are not the same and why some lessons should not transfer.

### Apple One vs Costco

Shared structure:
Both bundle multiple benefits behind a recurring payment and rely on the buyer believing the combined value exceeds the fee.

Critical difference:
Apple One bundles digital service entitlements within an ecosystem, while Costco monetizes membership access, retail savings, and in some tiers reward logic tied to purchase behavior.

Dangerous overgeneralization:
Treating both as the same membership bundle can hide the difference between digital entitlement consumption and retail access with savings realization.

Why distinction matters:
An Apple One transparency intervention may focus on service usage and subscription substitution. A Costco intervention may need to focus on renewal value, savings evidence, reward eligibility, and shopping behavior.

### Uber Surge vs HP Instant Ink

Shared structure:
Both can change what a buyer pays based on conditions outside a simple fixed-price purchase.

Critical difference:
Uber surge changes price in response to real-time marketplace demand and supply pressure. HP Instant Ink changes cost exposure through plan allowances, page usage, rollover, and overage logic.

Dangerous overgeneralization:
Calling both "usage-sensitive pricing" may hide the difference between real-time market clearing and subscription allowance management.

Why distinction matters:
Surge visibility interventions must account for supply response and demand timing. Instant Ink interventions must account for usage predictability, allowance fit, overage anxiety, and plan switching.

### Shopify vs Airbnb

Shared structure:
Both support commerce between sellers and buyers and monetize platform-enabled economic activity.

Critical difference:
Shopify primarily sells merchant enablement tools and platform services, while Airbnb operates a lodging marketplace with guest-host matching, trust, service fees, and transaction governance.

Dangerous overgeneralization:
Treating both as generic marketplace monetization can erase the difference between SaaS-like merchant infrastructure and two-sided marketplace transaction economics.

Why distinction matters:
Fee, bundle, and intervention reasoning must track whether the platform monetizes tools for sellers, transactions between parties, demand generation, trust infrastructure, or a mix of these.

## Retrieval Quality Evaluation

Retrieval usefulness is not whether a case sounds similar. Retrieval usefulness is whether the retrieved case improves reasoning clarity.

Retrieval success signals:

- The retrieved case clarifies the current case's bill-changing logic.
- The retrieved case exposes a causal difference that limits analogy.
- The retrieved case helps name who pays, who benefits, and what risk moves.
- The retrieved case improves intervention trade-off reasoning.
- The retrieved case reveals whether a mapping is strong, partial, contrastive, or low-fit.

Retrieval failure signals:

- The retrieved case shares surface vocabulary but not causal structure.
- The retrieved case encourages a stronger analogy than the evidence supports.
- The retrieved case hides the current case's distinctive bill trigger.
- The retrieved case makes the explanation sound clever without improving discrimination.
- The retrieved case pushes reviewers toward a premature ontology label.

Analogy inflation risk:
A weak comparison can become inflated when repeated in roadmap, review, or lookup language. The Brain should preserve low-fit or contrastive status when a comparison is useful mainly because it shows what not to transfer.

Surface similarity vs causal similarity:
Surface similarity asks whether cases look alike. Causal similarity asks whether the same pricing logic changes the bill, moves risk, affects buyer behavior, and alters seller economics.

Archetype misuse risk:
An archetype should organize reasoning, not replace it. If an archetype makes reviewers skip mechanism analysis, it is harming the Brain even when the label sounds plausible.

## Intervention Simulation Reality Testing

Intervention protocols must survive realistic trade-off pressure. A simulation should name upside, downside, hidden trade-off, and possible false causal assumption before it is treated as useful reasoning.

### Costco Renewal Framing

Expected upside:
Members with visible realized value may renew with more confidence because the fee is evaluated against concrete savings, rewards, or benefits.

Expected downside:
Low-use members may cancel or downgrade when the recap makes weak value realization more visible.

Hidden trade-off:
Transparency can improve trust while reducing renewal from members who previously renewed through inertia.

Possible false causal assumption:
The Brain may assume that clearer value framing increases retention for all members, when the effect likely differs by usage segment.

### Uber Surge Visibility

Expected upside:
Clearer surge explanation may improve perceived fairness and help riders decide whether to wait, accept, or choose alternatives.

Expected downside:
More visible surge logic may increase price resistance, reduce demand at peak moments, or draw attention to fairness concerns.

Hidden trade-off:
Reducing surprise can also reduce seller flexibility if buyers anchor against the non-surge price.

Possible false causal assumption:
The Brain may assume that transparency improves trust without reducing marketplace-clearing effectiveness or supply incentives.

### Apple One Transparency

Expected upside:
Clearer bundle value, service usage, and substitution logic may help households understand whether the bundle is worth keeping.

Expected downside:
Transparency may reveal unused services and increase downgrades or unbundling.

Hidden trade-off:
The bundle's perceived value may depend partly on optionality, not only measured usage.

Possible false causal assumption:
The Brain may assume that listing all included services increases perceived value, when it may instead highlight unused entitlements.

### Snowflake Commitment Pricing

Expected upside:
Clearer commitment pricing may improve budget predictability and help customers align expected consumption with discount economics.

Expected downside:
Commitment clarity may make buyers more aware of lock-in, underuse risk, or forecasting uncertainty.

Hidden trade-off:
Commitment discounts can improve seller revenue visibility while moving demand forecast risk to the buyer.

Possible false causal assumption:
The Brain may assume that lower effective unit prices are always attractive, when buyers may value flexibility more than discount depth.

## Brain Failure Archive

Future Brain work should consider a documentation area:

```text
brain/failures/
```

This area should record repeated reasoning failures such as:

- failed analogies
- ontology failures
- overclaim
- false transfer
- retrieval failure
- intervention failure
- concept drift
- governance failure

Reasoning systems learn more from repeated failures than from clean examples. Clean examples show how the Brain should work when fit is good. Failure records show where fit broke, where reviewers over-transferred, where retrieval sounded useful but was not, and where governance failed to stop premature confidence.

This roadmap recommends the future documentation area only. It does not create `brain/failures/` in v1.

## What Should NOT Happen Yet

This phase should not trigger:

- ontology expansion from isolated cases
- production schema integration
- intelligent recommendation engine
- automated ontology retrieval
- intervention recommendation engine
- ontology-assisted public UI
- automatic pattern suggestion
- dynamic navigator logic

The Brain system has not yet survived enough real-case pressure. Until repeated stress tests show durable reasoning value, difficult cases should create evidence, failures, candidate gaps, and review questions rather than production features.

## Success Criteria

This phase succeeds when:

- weak analogies become visible
- low-fit mappings stay low-fit
- ontology gaps repeat consistently before expansion
- retrieval improves reasoning instead of sounding clever
- intervention simulations expose trade-offs honestly
- reviewers can explain why similar-looking systems are actually different
- governance survives repeated edge-case pressure

## Risks And Guardrails

| Risk | Why Dangerous | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ontology inflation | Every awkward case can become pressure for a new label, making the ontology larger but not clearer. | Treat isolated awkwardness as a candidate gap or failure record until repeated cases show the same unresolved problem. |
| analogy theater | Comparisons can sound insightful while hiding bill-trigger, risk-transfer, or buyer-control differences. | Require every comparison to state shared structure, critical difference, dangerous overgeneralization, and why the distinction matters. |
| elegant-but-useless abstraction | A concept can be clean enough to explain the architecture while too broad to improve case reasoning. | Judge concepts by whether they improve discrimination, retrieval, and intervention honesty across cases. |
| production pressure too early | Useful Brain language can leak into schema, UI, hidden JSON, automation, or public pages before it is stable. | Keep this phase documentation-only and require separate review before any production promotion. |
| false confidence from clean examples | Good-fit examples can make governance seem stronger than it is. | Include messy, low-fit, hybrid, and contrast cases that force reviewers to preserve uncertainty. |
| overfitting to known cases | The Brain can become optimized around familiar examples rather than durable pricing reasoning. | Stress test against unfamiliar systems, non-examples, and cases with different market structure. |
| governance bloat | New protocols can accumulate faster than reasoning improves. | Pressure-test existing governance first and create no new protocol unless a repeated gap cannot be handled by current layers. |

This phase pressure-tests existing and architecture-referenced Brain systems rather than replacing them.

Some referenced systems are not present in the repository yet. When a referenced file is absent, it remains roadmap context only and should not be treated as active governance, an implementation prerequisite, or evidence that the layer has already been operationalized.

- `pricing_reasoning_ontology.md` is pressure-tested for concept boundaries, low-fit mappings, and candidate gaps.
- `ontology_fit_classification.md` is pressure-tested for whether reviewers can preserve weak fit without forcing a clean mapping.
- `brain_lookup_retrieval_policy.md` is pressure-tested for whether retrieval improves reasoning clarity rather than surface similarity.
- `analogical_comparison_protocol.md` is pressure-tested for transfer discipline, contrast reasoning, and false analogy control.
- `pricing_intervention_simulation_protocol.md` is pressure-tested for trade-off honesty, evidence status, and intervention overclaim.
- `brain_review_board_v1.md` is pressure-tested for whether review decisions can block premature production use and request more stress tests.
- `brain_concept_lifecycle_governance_v1.md` is pressure-tested for whether repeated gaps are promoted slowly and isolated gaps remain exploratory.

If referenced governance or protocol files are absent from the checkout, they remain referenced architecture context only. This roadmap does not create, modify, or backfill those files.

This phase must not create or backfill missing ontology, retrieval, or analogical comparison files as a side effect of stress testing. Missing referenced files require separate review before creation.

## Suggested Execution Order

1. Costco
2. Uber surge pricing
3. Apple One
4. Snowflake
5. OpenAI API pricing
6. airline yield pricing
7. telecom bundle systems

This order gradually increases ontology pressure.

Costco starts with a relatively understandable membership and renewal system where bundle, reward, and value-recap reasoning can be tested without immediately introducing real-time repricing or multi-party marketplace logic.

Uber surge pricing adds dynamic repricing, time sensitivity, supply response, fairness concerns, and marketplace-clearing pressure.

Apple One returns to bundled entitlement reasoning but pressures the Brain to distinguish digital services, household access, substitution value, and unused entitlement visibility from Costco-style membership economics.

Snowflake adds hybrid commitment and usage pricing, where predictability, consumption growth, buyer forecast risk, and seller revenue visibility interact.

OpenAI API pricing adds model choice, usage metering, developer workflows, and the contrast between API consumption and ChatGPT Plus subscription access.

Airline yield pricing adds capacity perishability, inventory classes, timing, route demand, fare restrictions, and high public familiarity that can invite oversimplified analogy.

Telecom bundle systems combine recurring access, device financing, data allowances, promotional discounts, service boundaries, switching costs, and hidden fee exposure. They should come later because they pressure multiple Brain layers at once.
